# Learning Notes — Drug Discovery Fundamentals

## 1. Drug Discovery Lifecycle

Drug discovery is the systematic process of identifying and developing a potential medicine. It starts with understanding a disease and identifying a biological target, then progresses through finding and improving compounds, studying their safety and effectiveness, and eventually evaluating them in clinical studies and by regulatory authorities.

The simplified drug discovery lifecycle is:

Disease understanding
→ Target identification
→ Target validation
→ Hit identification
→ Hit-to-lead
→ Lead optimization
→ Preclinical evaluation
→ Clinical development
→ Regulatory evaluation

---

## 2. Target Identification

Target identification is the process of identifying a biological molecule, pathway, or mechanism that may be involved in a disease and could potentially be affected by a drug.

A target may be a protein such as a receptor, enzyme, ion channel, or other biological molecule.

Example:

Disease: Cancer

Possible target: EGFR

The identification of a target does not automatically prove that a drug acting on it will be effective.

---

## 3. Target Validation

Target validation is the process of determining whether changing the activity of a proposed target is likely to produce a useful therapeutic effect.

Researchers use biological and scientific evidence to determine whether the target is sufficiently connected to the disease.

Important distinction:

Target identification = "What biological target might be relevant?"

Target validation = "Is there enough evidence that affecting this target could help?"

---

## 4. Hit Identification

Hit identification is the process of finding molecules that show measurable interaction or activity against a biological target.

A molecule identified during this stage is called a "hit."

A hit is not automatically a drug candidate. It needs further investigation.

---

## 5. Hit-to-Lead

Hit-to-lead is the process of evaluating promising hits and selecting molecules that have characteristics suitable for further development.

Researchers investigate properties such as:

* Biological activity
* Potency
* Selectivity
* Chemical properties
* Early ADME characteristics
* Potential safety concerns

The goal is to identify promising lead compounds.

---

## 6. Lead Optimization

Lead optimization involves systematically improving a lead compound's properties.

Researchers may investigate whether changes to the molecule can improve:

* Potency
* Selectivity
* Pharmacokinetic properties
* ADME characteristics
* Safety profile

The objective is to obtain a compound with a stronger overall development profile.

---

## 7. Preclinical Evaluation

Preclinical evaluation occurs before a potential medicine is tested in humans.

It can involve laboratory and animal studies designed to investigate properties such as:

* Pharmacological activity
* Pharmacokinetics
* Pharmacodynamics
* Toxicology
* Safety

Preclinical evidence does not guarantee that a medicine will be successful in humans.

---

## 8. Pharmacokinetics

Pharmacokinetics (PK) describes what the body does to a drug.

The major processes are:

ADME

A — Absorption
D — Distribution
M — Metabolism
E — Excretion

For example, pharmacokinetics can describe how a drug enters the bloodstream, where it distributes, how it is metabolized, and how it is eliminated.

---

## 9. Pharmacodynamics

Pharmacodynamics (PD) describes what a drug does to the body.

It includes relationships between drug exposure and biological effects.

Examples include:

* Target interaction
* Biological response
* Dose-response relationships
* Therapeutic effects
* Adverse effects

Simple distinction:

PK = What the body does to the drug.

PD = What the drug does to the body.

---

## 10. Toxicology

Toxicology is the study of harmful or undesirable effects caused by substances.

During drug development, toxicology information helps researchers understand potential safety risks.

A compound showing promising activity may still be unsuitable for development if its safety profile is unacceptable.

---

## 11. Formulation

Formulation is the development of a suitable dosage form and composition for administering a drug.

Examples of dosage forms include:

* Tablets
* Capsules
* Injections
* Solutions

Formulation becomes especially important when considering how a potential medicine can be delivered effectively and consistently.

---

## 12. Clinical Development

Clinical development involves studying a potential medicine in human participants under controlled research conditions.

Clinical development is generally organized into phases that progressively evaluate safety, dosing, effectiveness, and other important characteristics.

A compound entering clinical development is not guaranteed to become an approved medicine.

---

## 13. Regulatory Evaluation

Regulatory evaluation is the assessment of evidence by a regulatory authority to determine whether a medicine meets applicable requirements for quality, safety, and effectiveness.

Regulatory approval is based on evidence submitted for the particular product and its proposed use.

---

## Important Research Principle

A compound appearing in a scientific database does not automatically mean that it is a validated therapeutic candidate.

For this project, I will distinguish:

1. Evidence directly supported by a source.
2. My analysis or interpretation of the evidence.
3. Information that is unavailable or uncertain.

When reliable information cannot be established, I will record:

UNKNOWN / NOT FOUND

I will not fill missing scientific information using assumptions.
# 3. Drug Discovery Glossary

## 3.1 Active Pharmaceutical Ingredient (API)

The active pharmaceutical ingredient is the substance in a medicine that produces the intended pharmacological effect.

Example:
In a tablet containing an active drug molecule and inactive ingredients, the drug molecule is the API.

---

## 3.2 Molecular Target

A molecular target is a biological molecule that a drug interacts with to produce or modify a biological effect.

Common types of targets include:

* Receptors
* Enzymes
* Ion channels
* Transporters

Example:
EGFR is a molecular target for several drugs used in certain types of cancer.

---

## 3.3 Binding

Binding refers to the interaction between a drug molecule and a biological target.

A drug may bind to a target through different types of molecular interactions.

Binding to a target does not by itself prove that the drug will produce a useful therapeutic effect.

---

## 3.4 Mechanism of Action (MoA)

Mechanism of action describes how a drug produces its biological or therapeutic effect.

Example:

Drug → binds/inhibits target → changes biological signaling → produces a biological effect.

The mechanism should be supported by scientific evidence rather than assumed from the target name alone.

---

## 3.5 Potency

Potency describes how much of a drug is required to produce a specified biological effect.

A commonly used measurement is IC50.

IC50 is the concentration of a substance required to produce 50% inhibition in a particular experimental assay.

Important:

IC50 values from different experimental systems should not automatically be treated as directly equivalent.

---

## 3.6 Selectivity

Selectivity describes how preferentially a compound affects its intended target compared with other biological targets.

A compound may have strong activity against its intended target but also interact with other targets.

Therefore, selectivity information is important when evaluating a compound.

---

## 3.7 Bioavailability

Bioavailability refers to the fraction of an administered dose that reaches systemic circulation in an available form.

For an orally administered drug, bioavailability can be affected by factors such as:

* Absorption
* Metabolism
* Food effects
* Formulation
* Chemical properties

---

## 3.8 ADME

ADME stands for:

A — Absorption
D — Distribution
M — Metabolism
E — Excretion

ADME describes important processes that influence the movement and fate of a drug in the body.

### Absorption

How the drug enters systemic circulation.

### Distribution

How the drug moves from the bloodstream into tissues and other parts of the body.

### Metabolism

How the body chemically modifies the drug, often through enzymes.

### Excretion

How the drug and/or its metabolites are removed from the body.

---

## 3.9 Toxicity

Toxicity refers to harmful or undesirable effects caused by a substance.

A compound can have strong biological activity and still be unsuitable for development because of safety concerns.

Therefore:

High potency ≠ automatically safe.

---

## 3.10 Pharmacokinetics (PK)

Pharmacokinetics describes what the body does to a drug.

It includes processes such as:

* Absorption
* Distribution
* Metabolism
* Excretion

PK information can include concentration over time, exposure, half-life, clearance, and other measurements.

---

## 3.11 Pharmacodynamics (PD)

Pharmacodynamics describes what a drug does to the body.

It examines relationships between drug exposure and biological effects.

Examples include:

* Target engagement
* Biological response
* Dose-response relationship
* Therapeutic effect
* Adverse effects

Simple rule:

PK = What the body does to the drug.

PD = What the drug does to the body.

---

## 3.12 Lead Compound

A lead compound is a molecule that shows sufficiently promising properties to justify further investigation and optimization.

A lead compound is not necessarily an approved medicine.

Researchers may modify a lead compound to improve its:

* Potency
* Selectivity
* ADME properties
* Pharmacokinetic properties
* Safety characteristics

---

## 3.13 Candidate Compound

A candidate compound is a compound selected for further evaluation because the available evidence supports its potential for continued development or investigation.

The exact meaning can depend on the stage of research and the context.

For this project, the term "candidate compound" refers to a compound selected for systematic evidence collection and comparison.

It does not mean that the compound is proven to be clinically superior.

---

## 3.14 Evidence

Evidence is information supported by a credible source, such as:

* Peer-reviewed scientific publications
* Regulatory documents
* Established biomedical databases
* Clinical trial records

Evidence should be traceable to its source.

---

## 3.15 Evidence Confidence

Evidence confidence represents how confidently a particular piece of information can be supported by the available sources.

For this project, evidence confidence may be recorded as:

High — Strong and directly relevant supporting evidence.

Medium — Evidence exists but has limitations, indirectness, or incomplete support.

Low — Limited or uncertain evidence.

UNKNOWN / NOT FOUND — The required information could not be established from the sources reviewed.

---

## 3.16 Evidence Gap

An evidence gap is important information that is missing, insufficient, uncertain, or conflicting.

Examples:

* Potency information is unavailable.
* Human safety evidence is limited.
* Selectivity has not been adequately characterized.
* Different studies report different results.

An evidence gap should not be converted into a positive assumption.

---

## 3.17 Research Priority

Research priority describes what should be investigated next based on the current evidence and evidence gaps.

For example:

If a compound has strong target evidence but limited safety information, additional safety evidence may become a research priority.

A research priority is a recommendation based on the evidence; it is not proof that the compound is superior.

---

# 4. Key Distinctions

## Potency vs Selectivity

Potency asks:

"How much compound is needed to produce a specified effect?"

Selectivity asks:

"How preferentially does the compound affect the intended target compared with other targets?"

---

## PK vs PD

PK:

What the body does to the drug.

PD:

What the drug does to the body.

---

## Target Identification vs Target Validation

Target identification:

Find a biological target that may be relevant to a disease.

Target validation:

Determine whether there is sufficient evidence that influencing that target could produce a useful therapeutic effect.

---

## Lead Compound vs Candidate Compound

Lead compound:

A promising molecule that is being investigated and optimized.

Candidate compound:

A compound selected for more focused evaluation based on the evidence available at that stage.

---

## Evidence vs Analysis

Evidence:

What the source directly supports.

Analysis:

Our interpretation of the available evidence.

These must not be presented as if they are the same thing.

---

# 5. Scientific Research Rule

The most important rule for this project is:

"Do not convert missing information into positive information."

If a source does not provide reliable information about a property, record:

UNKNOWN / NOT FOUND

Do not estimate, guess, or invent the value.
# 6. Evidence-Based Drug Discovery Research Workflow

## 6.1 Define the Research Question

Before collecting data, the research question should be clearly defined.

A good research question identifies:

* Disease or therapeutic area
* Biological target or mechanism when appropriate
* Type of compounds being investigated
* Evidence that needs to be collected

Example research question:

"Among publicly documented compounds associated with EGFR-mutated non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC), what evidence exists for their target, mechanism, potency, selectivity, ADME, safety, and development stage?"

---

## 6.2 Define the Research Scope

The research scope determines what will and will not be investigated.

For this project, the scope should include:

* Publicly available scientific information
* Candidate compound information
* Molecular targets
* Mechanisms of action
* Potency
* Selectivity
* ADME
* Safety/toxicity
* Development stage
* Supporting publications
* Evidence gaps

The project does not include:

* Compound synthesis
* Laboratory experiments
* Administration of compounds
* Manufacturing
* Clinical treatment recommendations

---

## 6.3 Identify Credible Sources

Scientific information should be collected from credible sources.

Examples include:

* PubChem
* ChEMBL
* PubMed
* Europe PMC
* ClinicalTrials.gov
* FDA
* Protein Data Bank
* WHO resources where relevant

Different sources provide different types of information.

For example:

A chemical database may be useful for compound identity and molecular information, while a regulatory source may provide important information about approved uses and safety warnings.

---

## 6.4 Collect Evidence

For each candidate, collect information from the selected sources.

Example:

Candidate:
Gefitinib

Target:
EGFR

Mechanism:
EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibition

Evidence stage:
Approved

Other properties:
Potency, selectivity, ADME, safety, publications, etc.

Every important scientific statement should have a traceable source.

---

## 6.5 Record the Evidence

The information should be stored in a structured format rather than only in notes.

For example:

| Field               | Example           |
| ------------------- | ----------------- |
| Candidate ID        | C-001             |
| Compound name       | Example compound  |
| Target              | Example target    |
| Mechanism           | Example mechanism |
| Evidence stage      | Approved          |
| Potency             | Reported          |
| Selectivity         | Reported          |
| ADME                | Reported          |
| Safety              | Reported          |
| Evidence confidence | High              |

If reliable information cannot be found:

`UNKNOWN / NOT FOUND`

---

## 6.6 Compare Candidates

After collecting information for multiple compounds, candidates can be compared using predefined evidence-based criteria.

Possible comparison areas:

* Target relevance
* Mechanism
* Potency
* Selectivity
* ADME evidence
* Safety evidence
* Research evidence
* Development stage
* Evidence completeness

The comparison must be based on documented evidence.

---

## 6.7 Identify Evidence Gaps

After comparison, determine what information is missing or uncertain.

Examples:

* Missing potency information
* Limited selectivity data
* Limited human safety information
* Conflicting results between studies
* Limited clinical evidence
* Missing ADME information

These gaps become part of the Evidence Gap Report.

---

## 6.8 Determine Research Priorities

Evidence gaps can be used to determine what researchers should investigate next.

Example:

Known:
Strong target evidence.

Unknown:
Limited safety evidence.

Research priority:
Investigate additional safety evidence.

This does not mean the compound is unsafe or safe. It means that the available evidence is insufficient to answer the question confidently.

---

## 6.9 Produce the Final Research Output

The complete workflow is:

Research question
↓
Research scope
↓
Credible sources
↓
Evidence collection
↓
Candidate registry
↓
Evidence validation
↓
Candidate comparison
↓
Evidence-gap analysis
↓
Research priorities
↓
Structured final report

---

# 7. Evidence Hierarchy

Not all sources provide the same type or strength of evidence.

A simplified hierarchy for this project is:

### Stronger evidence

* Regulatory documents
* Peer-reviewed primary research
* Clinical trial records
* Well-established biomedical databases with documented provenance

### Supporting evidence

* Systematic reviews
* Review articles
* Institutional scientific resources

### Discovery/supporting information

* Educational webpages
* General summaries
* Search-engine results

Search engines are useful for finding sources, but the search result itself should generally not be treated as the scientific evidence.

---

# 8. Source Triangulation

When possible, important information should be checked across more than one credible source.

Example:

Source A:
Reports the compound's molecular identity.

Source B:
Reports the target.

Source C:
Provides clinical or regulatory information.

Together, these provide stronger evidence than relying on one source for everything.

However, different sources may contain different experimental conditions or definitions. Differences should be documented rather than automatically treated as errors.

---

# 9. Important Rule for This Project

The purpose of the pipeline is not to prove:

"Candidate X is the best drug."

The purpose is to determine:

"What does the available evidence currently show about each candidate?"

Therefore, the final analysis should distinguish:

Evidence
→ information supported by sources.

Analysis
→ interpretation of the collected evidence.

Unknown
→ information that could not be established.

Research priority
→ what should be investigated next.
# 10. Evidence Evaluation Principles

For this project, scientific information will be evaluated according to its source, relevance, directness, and completeness.

The following principles will be followed:

1. A database record does not automatically establish therapeutic validity.

2. Important scientific claims should be traceable to credible sources.

3. Primary research can provide direct experimental evidence for specific findings.

4. Review articles are useful for understanding the field but may summarize evidence from other studies.

5. Regulatory documents can provide important evidence about approved products, clinical information, and safety.

6. Different sources may report different results because experimental conditions can differ.

7. Conflicting evidence should be documented rather than hidden.

8. Missing information should be recorded as UNKNOWN / NOT FOUND.

9. Evidence and analytical interpretation must be clearly separated.

10. A research ranking does not mean that the highest-ranked compound is clinically superior.

11. Strong potency alone does not establish overall drug-development suitability.

12. The purpose of this project is to organize and evaluate publicly available evidence, not to claim that a new medicine has been discovered.
